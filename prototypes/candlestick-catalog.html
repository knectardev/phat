A Catalog of Candlestick Reversal Patterns
with Volume & L2-Ghost Counterparts

Part I: Fifteen canonical forms from the Japanese tradition, each paired with its volume-weighted analogue. Part II: The L2-ghost overlay — how the order-book state beneath the candle reveals a layer of conviction no traditional pattern can see.
Plates I — XV · Scenarios A — K

The candlestick patterns codified by Sakata and brought into the Western canon by Steve Nison presume a chart in which every bar is drawn to the same width. They are a taxonomy of body and wick geometry — a vocabulary developed before the widespread visual integration of volume into the candle itself. This catalog places each canonical pattern beside its volume-weighted counterpart, in which the candle's width is proportional to its relative volume.

The aim is not to displace the traditional vocabulary but to annotate it. In most cases, the volume dimension refines a signal the traditional pattern already points toward. In a few cases, it produces a meaningful subdivision — a single name in the Japanese canon splitting into several behaviorally distinct events.

Each entry notes its closest cousin in the volume-weighted coordinate system (the matrix of Effort × Result) that this reference is a companion to. The two vocabularies are complementary, not competing.

The Hammer — Takuri

“Probing the depths with the foot”
Canonical · Equal-Width
Small body at the top, long lower shadow twice the body length
Volume-Weighted
The authentic hammer: the small body carries heavy volume — sellers pushed, but were absorbed

A classic bullish reversal at the end of a downtrend. Price opens, is driven down to a new low by sellers, then recovers strongly to close near the open. Nison emphasizes three conditions: a small real body near the top of the range, a lower shadow at least twice the body's length, and little or no upper shadow. The body color is secondary, though a white (bullish) body is marginally stronger.

The psychology is one of failed capitulation — a final flush that found buyers waiting beneath.

What the Width Adds

A hammer with wide volume is a hammer in the fullest sense — visible effort was expended to drive price down, and that effort was absorbed. This is the textbook reversal.

A hammer with narrow volume — identical body-and-wick geometry but anemic participation — is a different animal. It may be a liquidity gap low rather than genuine absorption. Treat as a weak signal requiring confirmation.

The Hanging Man

“The gallows at the peak”
Canonical · Equal-Width
Geometrically identical to the hammer — small body, long lower shadow — but appearing at a peak
Volume-Weighted
Heavy volume with a small body at the peak — distribution at resistance

The hanging man has the same anatomy as the hammer but appears after an uptrend. The market rallied, was then pushed down sharply, and recovered — but the very fact that sellers could drive price that far below the open signals that the buying has weakened. Nison is careful: this pattern requires bearish confirmation on the following bar.

Without confirmation it is simply an ambiguous candle at a peak. The body color matters less than the length of the lower shadow relative to the body.

What the Width Adds

A wide hanging man is the telling version — the heavy volume means sellers genuinely appeared and made a visible incursion, even if buyers recovered the day. That is exactly the early-distribution pattern traders are watching for.

A narrow hanging man, by contrast, may simply be low-conviction drift at the top of a range. It is more often noise than signal.

The Shooting Star

“A rejected ascent”
Canonical · Equal-Width
Small body near the low, long upper shadow at least twice the body length
Volume-Weighted
Wide body with long upper shadow — price was pushed up on real volume and rejected

Price opens, rallies strongly, and then falls back to close near the open. The upper shadow marks the territory that sellers reclaimed. In Nison's framework the pattern requires a gap up from the prior body for full significance, though in practice traders accept gapless variants.

The shooting star is psychologically one of the clearest: the bulls reached for the stars and were rejected. What matters is not that price tried, but that it failed.

What the Width Adds

A wide shooting star tells you the rally was attempted with full conviction and still got rejected. That is a markedly stronger signal than the same geometry on thin volume, which may simply be the top of an illiquid rally.

Shooting stars on anemic volume often fall into the Vacuum quadrant — price traveled but no one participated. These patterns tend to mean-revert without needing the classic bearish confirmation.

The Inverted Hammer

“A first attempt, rejected”
Canonical · Equal-Width
Same anatomy as shooting star but appearing at a trough
Volume-Weighted
The wide version implies a real test of higher prices was made — buyers showed up once

A cousin of the shooting star, differentiated only by its position in a downtrend rather than an uptrend. The inverted hammer shows that buyers made a probing attempt to rally before being pushed back. It is a softer signal than the hammer proper — Nison requires strong bullish confirmation the next day.

Think of it as a first skirmish. The bulls have announced their presence, but they have not yet won.

What the Width Adds

Volume makes the inverted hammer far more legible. A wide inverted hammer signals that buyers mustered genuine participation in their attempted rally — the fact that sellers beat it back is bearish, but the presence of real buying is the reversal seed.

A narrow inverted hammer is often ambiguous enough to dismiss.

The Doji — Dōji

“A mistake, or: simultaneous”
Canonical · Equal-Width
Open equals close (or nearly so). The body is a horizontal line. Upper and lower shadows may be long or short.
Volume-Weighted
The wide doji is the Wyckoff “squat” — equal effort from both sides, a profound balance

Open and close occur at effectively the same price. The doji is the most explicit single-bar signal of indecision in the Japanese canon. Its significance depends entirely on context: a doji after a strong trend is a warning; a doji in a range is noise.

Nison treats the doji less as a pattern and more as a modifier — a doji inside a larger multi-bar pattern often amplifies that pattern's significance (e.g., the Morning Doji Star).

What the Width Adds

The volume-weighted dimension splits the doji into two fundamentally different events:

The narrow doji is disinterest. Neither side committed. This is the Apathy quadrant proper — a coiled spring, possibly.

The wide doji is perfect battle. Both sides brought their full weight and reached a dead draw. This is the Wall quadrant. Wide dojis after strong trends are among the most reliable reversal signatures in the entire taxonomy — more reliable, arguably, than most named multi-bar patterns.

Gravestone & Dragonfly Doji

“Tōba” · “Tonbo”
Canonical · Gravestone
Open = low = close. Long upper shadow. A tombstone silhouette.
Canonical · Dragonfly
Open = high = close. Long lower shadow. An insect at rest.

Special dojis in which one of the shadows is effectively zero. The gravestone — open, low, and close all at the day's low — forms at peaks and is a strong bearish signal; the bulls pushed up all day, achieved nothing, and closed back at the starting point. The dragonfly is the mirror image at troughs.

Both are among the most visually unambiguous candles in the canon.

What the Width Adds

Width turns these from curious single-bar signals into high-conviction events. A wide gravestone is a perfect expression of absorption at resistance: heavy volume, maximum attempted range, zero net result — the canonical Wall.

A narrow gravestone often represents a low-liquidity false probe and is less reliable. The shape is less informative than the shape + volume together.

The Marubozu — Marubōzu

“Shaven head, no shadow”
Canonical · Equal-Width
No upper or lower shadow — open at the low, close at the high
Volume-Weighted
Wide + tall = Arms' power box, Wyckoff's perfect conviction

A candle with a full-length body and no shadows at all. The market opened, moved decisively in one direction for the entire session, and closed at the extreme. The Marubozu is the purest expression of one-sided conviction in the canon.

When one appears after a consolidation, it often signals the breakout direction. Multiple consecutive Marubozu bars constitute Three White Soldiers or Three Black Crows (Plate XV).

What the Width Adds

A wide Marubozu is the matrix's Engine quadrant made flesh — maximum result achieved through maximum effort, without any contest. This is Arms' "power box" and is the single most reliable momentum signature across all candle forms.

A narrow Marubozu is rarer and more suspicious: full-range directional movement on light volume suggests a liquidity gap (Vacuum), not genuine conviction. Watch for snap-back.

Bullish Engulfing

“Tsutsumi ashi” — “the wrapped leg”
Canonical · Equal-Width
A small bearish candle followed by a large bullish candle whose body fully covers the prior
Volume-Weighted
The authentic engulfing: thin-volume sell capitulates; heavy-volume buy confirms

A small bearish candle inside a downtrend is followed by a bullish candle whose real body completely engulfs the prior body. The longer the engulfing candle relative to the engulfed, the more significant. Nison considers this one of the most reliable reversal signals.

The psychology is a sudden shift in control: the bears' weak offering is met with an overwhelming bullish response.

What the Width Adds

This is where the volume dimension is most diagnostic. The classic engulfing pattern splits cleanly into three distinct events:

Narrow-red engulfed by wide-green — The textbook case. The selling was weak, the buying is strong. This is the reversal in its purest form.

Wide-red engulfed by wide-green — A true battle. The bears showed up but the bulls overwhelmed them. Strongest variant; often marks a decisive swing low.

Wide-red engulfed by narrow-green — A geometric engulfing that is substantively a trap. The bulls barely participated. Fails frequently.

Bearish Engulfing

Mirror of the bullish engulfing
Canonical · Equal-Width
A small bullish candle engulfed by a large bearish candle
Volume-Weighted
Thin-volume buying exhausts; heavy-volume selling takes control

At the top of an uptrend, a small bullish candle is followed by a bearish candle whose body fully engulfs the prior. This is one of the most reliable bearish reversal signals in the canon. Like its bullish counterpart, it benefits from gaps and extended prior trends.

What the Width Adds

The same three variants apply as with bullish engulfing. The strongest case is a narrow-green bar (fading buying) engulfed by a wide-red bar (decisive selling). A narrow-red engulfing a wide-green bar looks structurally dramatic but is often a liquidity-gap reversal that fails to follow through.

The Piercing Line — Kirikomi

“A cutting into”
Canonical · Equal-Width
Long red, then green opens lower and closes above the red's midpoint
Volume-Weighted
The second bar shows heavy conviction — buyers arrived in force

A long bearish candle is followed by a bullish candle that opens at a new low (or gaps down) and then closes at least halfway up into the body of the prior bar. The deeper the piercing, the stronger the signal. A piercing that closes above the prior open becomes a Bullish Engulfing.

The Piercing Line is weaker than a full engulfing but more common, especially in intraday settings where true gaps are rare.

What the Width Adds

The credibility of a piercing line hinges on whether the bulls showed up with real weight. A piercing candle on heavy volume is functionally close to a Bullish Engulfing — the geometric technicality matters less than the participation.

On thin volume, the pattern often produces a dead-cat bounce that retraces within a few bars.

Dark Cloud Cover — Kabuse

“Covering over”
Canonical · Equal-Width
Long green, then red opens higher and closes below the green's midpoint
Volume-Weighted
Heavy selling after the cheerful green — distribution into strength

The bearish counterpart to the Piercing Line. A long bullish candle is followed by a bearish candle opening at a new high and closing below the midpoint of the prior body. The deeper the penetration, the stronger the signal.

What the Width Adds

As with the Piercing Line, the character of the reversal bar's volume tells most of the story. Heavy-volume dark cloud is distribution in action. Thin-volume dark cloud may just be profit-taking that resolves upward again.

The Harami — Harami

“Pregnant”
Canonical · Equal-Width
A large body with a small opposite-color body nestled entirely inside its range — “the mother with child”
Volume-Weighted
Wide mother (conviction) followed by narrow child (exhaustion) — the trend's breath is held

A long-bodied candle is followed by a small-bodied candle of the opposite color whose body is entirely contained within the prior body. It is the opposite of an engulfing: rather than the second candle swallowing the first, the second candle is swallowed.

The Harami suggests the prevailing trend has paused — not reversed, but lost conviction. Nison treats it as a weaker reversal signal than engulfing but notes that the Harami Cross (where the inside candle is a doji) is a much stronger pattern.

What the Width Adds

The volume dimension is diagnostic here. A proper Harami should show a wide mother (strong trend bar) followed by a narrow child (dwindling participation) — this is the textbook exhaustion signature.

If the child is wide instead, you do not have a Harami at all — you have a battle bar that merely happens to sit within the mother's range. That is a very different event.

Tweezers Top & Bottom

“Matched shadows”
Canonical · Tweezers Top
Two candles that touch the same high — tweezer-like twin peaks
Canonical · Tweezers Bottom
Two candles that touch the same low — a double-bottom miniature

Two consecutive candles that reach the same extreme price (the same high at a top, or the same low at a bottom). Tweezers are a minor pattern on their own but gain significance when combined with other signals, or when the second candle exhibits a reversal character of its own (such as a doji or hammer).

What the Width Adds

Tweezers are fundamentally about rejection at a level twice. The volume dimension tells you how much participation was rejected. Two wide bars with matched extremes is a powerful double-absorption pattern — essentially two consecutive Wall quadrant candles at the same price, strongly implying institutional absorption.

Two narrow bars at the same extreme is less meaningful; it may simply be that no one tested the level.

Morning Star & Evening Star

“Sankawa ake no myōjō” · “Sankawa yoi no myōjō”
Morning Star · Bullish
Long red → small star (often a doji) → long green. A bottom forming over three sessions.
Evening Star · Bearish
Long green → small star → long red. A top forming over three sessions.

Among Nison's most highly regarded reversal patterns. The Morning Star begins with a long bearish candle, followed by a small-bodied candle that gaps down (the "star"), and concludes with a long bullish candle that closes well into the body of the first. The Evening Star is the mirror image at a top. When the star itself is a doji, the pattern becomes the Morning/Evening Doji Star, which is considered stronger still.

The three-bar pattern traces a narrative arc: exhaustion of the prior trend (bar 1), indecision and transfer of control (bar 2), and decisive reversal (bar 3).

What the Width Adds

The Morning/Evening Star maps almost perfectly onto a three-phase matrix path: wide-trend bar → narrow star → wide reversal bar. The cleanest versions show this volume arc: heavy effort at the peak/trough, near-zero effort at the star (the market holds its breath), then heavy effort in the reversal direction.

Uniform-width versions in which all three bars carry similar volume are weaker — the narrative of "pause, then turn" is what the volume arc makes visible.

Three White Soldiers & Three Black Crows

“Aka sanpei” · “San-byō kabuse”
Three White Soldiers
Three consecutive long green bars, each opening inside the prior body and closing at a new high
Three Black Crows
Three consecutive long red bars stepping down in formation

Strong continuation patterns signaling that a trend has firmly established itself. Each of the three bars opens within the previous body and closes at a new high (or low). The pattern is most significant when appearing after a consolidation or a prior pattern that resolved directionally.

These are among the clearest patterns in the canon — no ambiguity, just sustained pressure in one direction over multiple sessions.

What the Width Adds

Three Soldiers on increasing volume is the canonical healthy trend — each bar commits more capital than the last. This is sustained Q2 Engine, the matrix's strongest continuation signature.

Three Soldiers on decreasing volume is a warning. The geometric pattern is the same, but the trend is being supported by fewer and fewer participants — a Q2-to-Q1 drift that often precedes a reversal. No traditional name exists for this distinction, but it is one of the most important volume-weighted refinements in practice.

The L2-Ghost Overlay

Beneath every executed candle lies an unexecuted one — the resting order book at the moment the interval began. When the two diverge, the alpha appears.
Primer · How to Read a Ghost Candle

The Two Overlays

The canonical candlestick tells us what happened during an interval — what price did while the clock ran. It cannot tell us about the resting state of the market: the queue of orders that stood waiting before the first trade printed, the depth stacked behind the bid and the ask, the structural intentions of participants who never got executed.

The Ghost Candle is that second overlay. It is rendered beneath the price candle as a translucent specimen, sharing the same center point but with different dimensions:

The Price Candle
Work done during the interval
Width
Executed volume — how much capital actually changed hands
Height
Body range — how far price traveled from open to close
Color
Execution pressure — green when buyers overwhelmed infrastructure, red when sellers did
Wicks
Rejected reality — prices that printed but could not hold
The Ghost Candle
State of the book at interval start
Width
L2 depth — how thick the resting queue was, independent of what executed
Height
Book thickness — how much depth stood behind the spread
Color
Book imbalance — cyan if bids were heavier, coral if asks, grey if balanced
Wicks
Structural guardrails — orders resting far from the spread

Coherence is the alignment between these two overlays. When the price candle and ghost candle broadly agree in size and color, the market is telling a single story — execution matches infrastructure, and what we see is what was intended. When they diverge, the market is telling two stories at once: the executed one and the hidden one. The divergent cases are where structural opportunity lives.

Part II catalogs the named scenarios that arise from specific combinations of price and ghost. The four key scenarios are the canonical extremes. The seven supplementary scenarios are variations worth recognizing by name in a live tape.

The Engine is the latter. It is the most persistent continuation signal in the augmented vocabulary; when both layers agree at extremity, the move tends to extend rather than exhaust.

A dramatic move that infrastructure cannot support
Dual-Layer · Divergent

price ghost

The price candle is dramatic — wide volume, long body, heavy color. But the ghost candle is a small shadow: when the interval began, the book was thin and neutrally balanced. There was no structural foundation for the move that followed.

In practice, The Slip is how flash events happen. A modest aggressor arrives at a moment when resting depth is unusually shallow. The trade prints at extremity not because a consensus shifted but because there was no floor to catch it. The ghost tells you the move was executed into a vacuum.

What the Ghost Adds

This scenario does not exist in the traditional vocabulary. A canonical Marubozu on a trader's chart looks triumphant — full conviction in a single direction. With the ghost overlay, the same candle can be revealed as a stop run through a hollow book, which is a fundamentally different event and warrants the opposite trade.

The Slip is the strongest mean-reversion setup in the augmented vocabulary. Price that traveled far without infrastructure must, statistically, return to where infrastructure exists.

Both overlays are narrow, small, and directionally neutral. Neither buyers nor sellers made a decisive incursion during the interval, and the book's standing state is symmetric. The candle is a compressed point.

Coherence is not a reversal signal — it is a preparation signal. Something important is often about to happen, but the direction is not yet encoded in either overlay. The longer Coherence persists, the more decisive its eventual breakout tends to be. Wyckoff called this the spring loading; the prototype calls it the balanced state.

What the Ghost Adds

A narrow candle in isolation could mean anything. With the ghost overlay we can tell honest balance (both layers narrow, aligned, symmetric) from a compressed Slip or hidden accumulation — superficially similar-looking candles whose ghosts reveal very different underlying conditions.

True Coherence is the absence of both signal and noise. Watch for it; it is where the best setups gestate.

Hidden Floor — Silent Accumulation

A thin, weak-looking candle resting on a massive unseen bid
Dual-Layer · Divergent
price ghost
In the Wild
price ghost

The price candle is small, possibly bearish, unremarkable in traditional terms. But the ghost candle is enormous and decidedly bid-heavy. A giant queue of resting buy orders is stacked beneath the current price, though very little of that queue was touched during the interval.

This is the signature of silent institutional accumulation. Patient buyers have parked size; the tape is not yet rewarding them with executed trades, but they are present and they are not leaving. Price may linger or even dip further — but the floor is not where the price candle suggests; it is where the ghost is.

What the Ghost Adds

This is perhaps the clearest case where ghost analysis inverts the traditional reading. A small red candle on thin volume looks like nothing — Apathy or early weakness. With the ghost revealed, the same candle is recast as an iceberg bid: massive latent demand that has not yet forced itself onto the tape.

Hidden Floor is the bullish twin of The Slip. Where The Slip is dramatic execution over no infrastructure, Hidden Floor is minimal execution over massive infrastructure. Both are structural mispricings; both tend to resolve in favor of the ghost.

Appendix

The Volume-Weighted Matrix in Brief

For reference, the volume-weighted matrix referenced throughout this catalog partitions candlestick space into four quadrants based on two percentile-ranked dimensions:

Q1 · Vacuum
Low Volume · High Body Range
Price moved on anemic participation. Liquidity gap or stop run. Structurally hollow, frequently snaps back.
Q2 · Engine
High Volume · High Body Range
Effort and result in harmony. Institutional conviction, value migration, trend continuation's natural home.
Q3 · Apathy
Low Volume · Low Body Range
Neither side committed. The waiting room — but a prolonged residency is a coiled spring.
Q4 · Wall
High Volume · Low Body Range
Massive effort absorbed by a counter-party. The squat. Where trends die and reversals are born.

The named vectors describe transitions between quadrants over consecutive bars. Fake-out (Q3→Q1) is a breakout with no participation. Confirmation (Q1→Q2) is real money validating a thin move. Climax (Q2→Q4) is the trend hitting a wall. Exhaustion (Q4→Q3) is the spent market. Ignition (Q3→Q2) is the coiled spring releasing. Thinning (Q2→Q1) is momentum outpacing its fuel.

These vectors are the volume-weighted matrix's native vocabulary for the transitions that the Japanese canon names as multi-bar reversal patterns. Where the canon names the shape, the matrix names the motion.
